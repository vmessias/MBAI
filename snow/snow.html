In late summer 1854, the Soho district of London was struck by a sudden cholera outbreak that spread throughout August and early September, with cases concentrated around Broad Street. Within days, hundreds of deaths accumulated in the surrounding streets. For most physicians, the explanation seemed obvious: "miasma", air corrupted by filth, carried the disease through the alleys.

While nearly every British physician blamed ‘bad air’ from decomposing organic matter, Snow stood almost alone in arguing there was no correlation. Dismissed as ‘peculiar’ by London’s lead medical officer, he persisted—mapping deaths day by day to prove his theory...

John Snow (1813-1858)

John Snow was born on March 15, 1813, in York, England, and died on June 16, 1858, in London, at the age of 45. From humble origins, Snow became a physician and excelled in both epidemiology and anesthesiology.

Proximity Analysis: Deaths & Water Pumps

x1 Death

Snow then mapped every public water pump across the district. By dividing the region into proximity zones, assigning each household to its nearest pump, a pattern emerged: a striking concentration of deaths around the Broad Street pump.

Snow's spatial analysis revealed an undeniable pattern: nearly 70% of all deaths were clustered around the Broad Street pump. He investigated every exception to the rule, including the brewery where no workers fell ill, because they drank beer and had their own well. His analysis led to a strong hypothesis: contaminated water, not miasma, was the problem.

The Handle That Changed History

After nine days of investigation, John Snow convinced St. James Parish Board to remove the Broad Street pump handle on September 8. Though the epidemic was already subsiding (many residents had fled), no new cases emerged near the pump. But Snow’s battle for acceptance had only just begun!

The Paradox of Progress

1854–1858
Rejected

Snow's germ theory was dismissed by Victorian medical establishment. Miasma theory—the belief that "bad air" caused disease—dominated. He died in 1858 without seeing his theory accepted.

1866
Vindicated

London's final cholera outbreak struck areas not yet protected by new sewers. Snow's water contamination theory was finally proven correct—8 years after his death.

1884
Confirmed

Robert Koch’s work on Vibrio cholerae (first isolated in 1854) finally confirmed Snow’s hypothesis: cholera was a microscopic organism in contaminated water.

The Broad Street pump handle remains one of history's most powerful symbols: a single intervention, informed by spatial data, that saved many lives.
